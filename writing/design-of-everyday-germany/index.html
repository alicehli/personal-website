Designing for the Responsible Citizen

    Don Norman argues that good design makes the right action the easiest action. But during my last trip to Germany, I started to notice something his framework doesn’t quite capture: design doesn’t just shape what you do, it also encodes an assumption about who you are before you’ve done anything at all. Germany is the lens here because I know it well – nearly a dozen visits and family there – though many of these patterns appear across the EU, and the American counterexamples aren’t universal either.

    That encoded assumption is never neutral, it just isn’t always visible. A system that assumes you’ll comply and a system that assumes you’ll cheat not only produce different user experiences, but they produce different citizens. The more I observed around me, the more I realized that the most interesting question isn’t whether a system trusts you or doesn’t – it’s about how that system has calibrated that trust, and what happens to those who fall outside its model of the expected user.

    Social Contract of Waste: From Bottle Caps to Pfand

    I grew up in Vancouver, moved to France for college, and landed in New York City in my early twenties to start my career. Having lived in Canada and France, where recycling feels akin to a civic religion, moving to NYC gave me a bit of culture shock. It seemed like there was no cohesive, standardized recycling system. In New York, waste management felt like a battle against the city (just look at the streets…), but in Germany, it felt like a shared responsibility.

    One difference I noticed was in the “micro-designs” of German packaging. For instance, whenever we bought water, I noticed that each plastic lid is attached to the bottle by a stubborn, tiny cord of plastic. At first, it was a little annoying to drink from – it felt like the drinking equivalent of a lisp, with the cap constantly grazing my lip and water guzzling down my chin. But the design eventually won me over, because it was nice not having to intentionally account for where the lid was. There was a physical constraint tethering the cap and bottle to force a specific, desirable behavior and a clean mapping. Tens of billions of loose caps are used annually in the US, with bottle caps ranking among the most common items found in ocean and coastal cleanups (Ocean Conservancy, 2025). The EU mandated tethered caps under the Single-Use Plastics Directive (2019/904) effective 2024. In Germany, the system assumes that if you want the beverage, you assume the responsibility of the cap.

    I noticed micro-design in more fun places too. At a hotel breakfast, they had edible wafer cups for jam and honey. The container and its contents become one object – you eat the cup, and the waste problem disappears. The mapping is perfect because the correct behavior, to eat the cup, is also the most enjoyable one. But what I found the most ambitious example of German packaging design wasn’t about individual products – it was the entire recycling system.

    Germany’s Pfand system is a successful circular economy under the pretense of a recycling program. Consumers pay a small deposit (between 8 and 25 cents) which is refunded when the bottle is fed back into a reverse vending machine at the supermarket. Return rates for single-use containers exceed 97%, according to GVM, Germany’s packing market research body, largely eliminating the litter problem for beverage containers. The Pfand is what Thaler and Sunstein would call a nudge. Unlike informational nudges that dominate behavioral economics, such as default opt-ins, this nudge is built into physical infrastructure. The Pfand doesn’t tell you to recycle, but it makes recycling the obvious economic behavior, and trusts that you’re rational enough to follow a well-designed incentive without being forced.

    That said, the system isn’t perfect. Critics will say that the Pfand acts as a regressive levy, as deposit-refund systems combine a consumption tax with a recycling rebate, shifting the burden of unredeemed deposits to fall disproportionately to those who can’t easily trek back to a supermarket with a heavy bag of glass bottles (Ashenmiller, 2011). The energy required to transport and wash heavy reusable glass bottles sometimes offsets the carbon savings from not making new plastic. The energy math on glass is also genuinely contested (Coelho et al., 2020). But the behavioral shift it produces is worth the friction, because it transforms waste from an individual afterthought into a collective system.

    The Pfand also works in part because of unique environmental conditions: Germany is dense, walkable, and organized around frequent, proximate supermarkets with nearby return infrastructure. The system benefits from decades of cultural scaffolding around waste sorting that most Germans internalized from childhood. Transport the same scheme to suburban America, where the nearest return point might be a 15-minute drive, and the design stops being clever. A system that makes the right action easy is only as good as the environment that makes that action accessible.

    Despite these flaws, the Pfand remains a symbol of intentional social design. It transforms a piece of trash into a micro-currency, creating a social affordance where, if you’re in a park and you don’t want to carry your bottle home for a few cents, you can leave it beside the bin – not toss it in – so that a Flaschensammler can easily claim the deposit. The Pfand’s architects didn’t design this behavior. But the deposit incentive and existing social norms interacted to produce it anyway – and its emergence might be the best evidence that the underlying design worked. This mechanical system has evolved into something of a social grace, assuming that the citizen isn’t just a user, but a steward of the system. (Ashenmiller also found that deposit systems increase income for very low-wage workers who collect other containers!).

    The Supermarket Sprint

    The Pfand asks you to be a steward. The supermarket checkout asks something smaller but is built on the same logic. Germany is jokingly considered an uptight, orderly society, and that reputation extends to the way you check out your groceries. German supermarket checkouts feel efficient: you are responsible for quickly handling your own bagging, and the system is designed to ensure you don’t slow down the collective.

    The checkout counters often feature a split end – a “V” shape with a mechanical divider. Once your items are scanned and sent to one side of the “V”, the cashier flips a switch so that the next customer’s groceries begin piling on the other side. This split conveyor acts as a forcing function: it makes your side your responsibility, and the flipped divider is a signifier that your turn has begun. The moment the cashier flips the divider, a mild social clock starts ticking, and you’re expected to act your part. Unlike the tethered bottle cap, there’s no hard physical constraint here – just a social design nudge that makes the right behavior feel obvious.

    The supermarket checkout is probably the most benign version of the expert-user assumption running through all of these systems: the cost of failing here is low. If you fumble with your bags, at most, someone behind you might get annoyed. But the same underlying logic that produces a charming social clock at the checkout can scale into something much less forgiving. Germany’s everyday infrastructure runs on a spectrum from gentle social pressure to genuine institutional severity, and the design philosophy is the same across all of it: you are expected to already know the rules.

    Mobility without Barriers

    New York’s subway operates on a basic assumption: you will try to cheat the system. The design builds physical barriers to stop you – massive, expensive metal gates that are ironically still bypassable. For the determined, fare evasion is just part of an urban parkour routine. But Germany’s transit system operates on a fundamentally different wavelength: the honour system.

    When you walk onto a U-Bahn or S-Bahn platform in Berlin or Munich, there are no turnstiles, gates, or physical obstacles between you and the train. To an American eye, it feels almost illicit. Instead of a big, bulky barrier, there’s a small unassuming box on a pole – the Entwerter (validation machine). The design removes physical constraints entirely and relies on the user’s internalized model of how the system works. By eliminating the turnstile, the architecture allows hundreds of people to board and disembark without friction.

    In New York, the cost of designing for the adversarial user is high, and very annoying from a user perspective. Recently, I tapped my card at a busy station only to have seven people storm through the gate from the other side. My tap expired before I could move – I was still charged, but the gate wouldn’t budge. Then someone lunged in front of me with three cards on Apple Wallet (none of which worked), and a system designed for non-compliance cost me six dollars and eight minutes for the supposed crime of trying to catch a train. The system optimizes for preventing the worst-case user, and in doing so, seems to often degrade the experience for everyone else.

    However, these MTA designs aren’t just infrastructural stubbornness. The New York subway moves roughly 3.5 million riders on a weekday (MTA, 2024) across a system that spans enormous socio-economic diversity and geographic range. Fare evasion alone costs the agency close to a billion dollars each year (Citizens Budget Commission, 2025). Berlin’s U-Bahn serves a different demographic, with its own funding models and a distinct relationship between citizen and state. The MTA’s adversarial design is a response to legitimate operating conditions.

    The MTA’s approach still fails on its own terms. Norman distinguishes between slips, where a user intends the right action but the system causes an error, and mistakes, where the user’s mental model is itself incorrect. The gate system is designed to prevent mistakes – deliberate fare evasion – but in practice it can generate slips. My double charge wasn’t a system failure in the traditional sense. It was a well-intentioned user colliding with infrastructure optimized for a different user entirely. A system so focused on the adversarial case that it routinely punishes the compliant one has a design problem, regardless of how you feel about trust.

    In Germany, the system assumes you are a responsible participant who has already purchased a ticket and will do the socially responsible work of validating it.

    But this trust comes with a sharp edge. The feedback for non-compliance is delayed and severe. In the MTA, the feedback loop is tight: you tap and the gate opens, or it doesn’t, and you know immediately where you stand. Germany’s honor system stretches that loop to the point of invisibility – you board the train, everything feels normal, and you have no way of knowing whether you’ve done something wrong until a plainclothes Kontrolleur taps you on the shoulder. This is a gulf of evaluation in Norman’s terms: a gap between the system’s state and the user’s understanding of it.

    My mom experienced this firsthand. Her ticket had barely expired without us realizing, and the resulting sixty-euro fine was swift and non-negotiable. When she offered to pay on the spot – the most intuitive resolution for a tourist who knows she owes money – the officer refused. Instead, she was directed to a physical office on the other side of the city, where she would need to wait in line and pay by card.

    What’s revealing isn’t the fine itself but the recovery path. The system’s response to a user who failed to understand its rules was to demand even more fluency: navigate an unfamiliar city, locate an administrative office, understand its hours and payment conventions. The punishment for not understanding the system was dealing with more systems. This is a design pattern worth naming: the system’s trust in you doesn’t soften when you stumble – it escalates. And the escalation disproportionately burdens exactly the users who were least equipped to navigate the original interaction. For the expert user the system imagines, none of this is a problem. For a tourist with an expired ticket she didn’t know had expired, the system’s trust looked more like a trap.

    There’s still an element of dignity in this high-trust, high-consequence system – at least for the user it was designed for. It’s built for an educated, expert user who knows the rules and follows them. The US designs to prevent the worst behavior, assuming you’ll skip the fare and spending millions on physical infrastructure to discourage that. Germany designs to facilitate the best. The expected value of being caught isn’t worth saving a couple of euros, but there is a psychological value of moving through your city without being treated like a suspect. The question is who gets to feel that dignity, and who falls through the gap.

    Infrastructure That Assumes You’ll Survive

    In Berlin, bike lanes are often physically separated from traffic – not just optimistically painted stripes between a car door and a bus, but infrastructure that puts something concrete between you and a several-ton vehicle. The US will paint a line on a road and call it a bike lane, hoping drivers will respect it. I’ve had some very close calls biking in NYC, which is supposedly one of the better cities for commute cycling in the country.

    The design philosophy difference is stark. American bike infrastructure is retrofitted – painted onto roads designed for cars, as an afterthought, leading drivers to be annoyed that they have fewer lanes for driving. German bike lanes feel like they were there from the beginning (because they were!). Germany nearly tripled its bikeway network from 1976 to 1995, building coordinated, separated infrastructure (Pucher & Dijkstra, 2003). Separated bike lanes are actually distrust-based designs applied to drivers. The system assumes drivers will hit cyclists if given the chance, so it builds a physical barrier. A similar philosophy to the MTA turnstile, but pointed at a different user.

    This is where the simple trust vs. distrust dichotomy breaks down – Germany doesn’t uniformly trust its citizens. It trusts you to validate your train ticket, but it doesn’t trust that you won’t have a lapse in attention behind the wheel. The Pfand trusts you to respond to an incentive, but the separated bike lane assumes you won’t respond to a painted line. The trust is scaled by the risk. This is a matter of calibration. Which users, in which contexts, with what stakes, can be trusted with what behaviors? And what does the system do when it gets that calibration wrong?

    Norman has a useful distinction here: knowledge in the head versus knowledge in the world. Trust-based designs, like the honor system transit, rely on knowledge in the head – the user must already understand how the system works before encountering it. Distrust-based systems, like separated bike lanes, put knowledge in the world – the physical barrier tells you everything you need to know regardless of what you already understand. The best German designs match the approach to the stakes: if the failure mode is a slow checkout line, knowledge in the head is fine. When the failure mode is a dead cyclist, you put that knowledge in the world. But German civic infrastructure doesn’t always get this matching right.

    What Germany gets right in this case is not a blanket commitment to trust but knowing which users to trust with which behavior. Safer designs produce not only safer cycling but more cycling – fewer cars, less congestion, lower emissions, and lower long-term healthcare costs from both accidents and sedentary lifestyles. As a result, this design choice is also a public health decision.

    When Trust Becomes the Test

    When I think back to my mom’s transit fine, I realize it wasn’t an anomaly, but rather epitomizes the operating logic of Germany’s civic infrastructure. The spectrum I’ve been describing – from the gentle social clock at the supermarket checkout to the sixty-euro fine on the U-Bahn – reaches an extreme at the Bürgeramt, Germany’s civic registration system. You must register your address within two weeks or face fines, appointments in major cities require aggressive persistence to secure, and the system offers little accommodation if you don’t already have a clear understanding of the rules.

    The people who encounter this system most urgently – immigrants, refugees, those who have arrived in a country whose language they may not speak and whose bureaucratic conventions they have no reason to know – are the ones the system demands the most fluency from while possessing the least. What results is a near-total gulf of execution: the gap between what the user wants to do (register, comply, participate) and the actions the system requires is so wide that the design effectively punishes the intention to comply. By the calibration logic Germany applies so well to its bike lanes – high stakes plus diverse users equals knowledge in the world, not knowledge in the head – the Bürgeramt is a failure on its own terms. There’s a tension between trust that empowers and trust that excludes, and it shows up across German-speaking policy more broadly.

    Germany’s organ donation system is opt-in: you must actively register as a donor. Austria, culturally similar, is opt-out: you are presumed to consent unless you register an objection. The effect on consent rates is stark – roughly 12% in Germany versus over 99% in Austria (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003) – though this reflects registration behavior, not actual transplants performed. The gap is a measure of what the default produces: in one system, inaction means no; in the other, inaction means yes.

    Organ donation is the purest distillation of the design question running through this piece. Organ donation pushes the gulf of evaluation concept past its original scope: the gulf isn’t just wide, it’s nonexistent as a feedback channel. You will not receive a signal that your inaction has consequences, because those consequences are borne entirely by strangers you will never meet. This is not a gap in the feedback, it’s a total absence of it. The system trusts your autonomy, but most people, when surveyed, say they would donate. The intended behavior and the required action don’t match. Unlike the Pfand, where the deposit nudges you across that gap, the opt-in system offers no bridge at all. It respects your right to do nothing, and counts that cost in organs that won’t be donated.

    Austria’s opt-out design is a different calibration of trust. It trusts that most people’s inaction reflects inertia, not conviction, and designs the default accordingly. Thaler and Sunstein would call this libertarian paternalism: the system assumes what you’d probably want and makes that the path of least resistance, while preserving your right to override it. Germany applies this logic brilliantly to bottles and inconsistently to bodies.

    This is where philosophy reaches its limit. Trust-based design works when the cost of inaction is personal – you lose your own bottle deposit, you get fined for not validating your ticket. It falters when the cost is diffuse and borne by strangers. Someone else doesn’t get an organ because the system respects your autonomy to not actively choose. The design question becomes: when does trusting a user serve as respect, and when does it outsource moral responsibility onto individuals who would predictably not act?

    The same question running through this essay – “What does the default assume about you?” – reaches its limit here. The most consequential design choice isn’t what the system asks you to do. It’s what it assumes you’ve already decided.

    Designing for the Expected User

    These design questions aren’t unique to Germany. American design tends to work from the opposite assumption – designing for non-compliance, building physical barriers against the worst-case user, and in doing so creating systems that are simultaneously harder to navigate honestly and easier to game creatively. Like the MTA gate that charges you twice, and bike lanes that are just suggestions. Not just failures of engineering, but failures of assumption.

    Good design makes the right action the easiest action. What Germany adds is a moral dimension: good design also feels like participation rather than submission. It doesn’t treat you like a problem to be managed. It treats you like a person who already knows what to do, and trusts that you’ll do it.

    But the evidence complicates this. The same philosophy that produces the elegant Pfand produces the punishing Bürgeramt. The same respect for autonomy that makes the U-Bahn feel dignified makes organ donation rates lag behind Austria’s. Trust-based design has a blindspot: it works beautifully for the user it was designed for, and it can be genuinely hostile to everyone else. The question isn’t whether to trust – it’s who your model of the “expected user” includes, and what happens to the people it doesn’t.

    A system that assumes you are responsible is a system that respects you. But respect alone doesn’t guarantee the right outcome. These are questions about how any system – civic, digital, institutional – models its users and distributes consequences. The best designs know when to trust – and they know who they leave out when they do. Germany is a place that feels like home despite the fact I’ve never lived there. Maybe that’s because the best of its designs treated me like I already belonged. But that feeling is itself a data point: I am educated, I speak a novice amount of the language, I understand the conventions from many visits. I am the expected user. The question this essay has been asking – what happens to those who aren’t – doesn’t have a comfortable answer, and design that mistakes its expected user for its every user will keep reproducing that gap.
